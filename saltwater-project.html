Engineering notebook / October 2026

Saltwater-activated
battery & motor.

Design and test plan. Fabrication, motor operation and measured results are pending.

The energy path

Reacting metal → electricity → optional motor rotation.

Inspired by an earlier school propulsion concept. This experiment uses saltwater as an electrolyte and does not implement the original article’s proposed self-powered engine.

01 / Define the experiment

Question: How does salt concentration affect power delivered by a zinc–copper saltwater cell? Motor operation is a later demonstration, not a guaranteed outcome. Zinc is consumed; saltwater is the electrolyte, not a free fuel.

02 / Gather materials

Small zinc and copper sheet electrodes, plastic cups, salt, water, scale, ruler, insulated clip leads, multimeter, and 100 Ω and 1 kΩ resistors rated at least 0.25 W. An optional low-current educational motor needs published voltage/current specifications. Use eye protection and a spill tray.

03 / Design the holder

Sketch parallel electrodes about 10 mm apart with equal exposed area and no contact. CAD and 3D-print a cup-rim holder that keeps the clips dry. Dry-fit first; record spacing and exposed area.

04 / Prepare solutions

Use separate cups containing 0, 1 and 3.5 g salt per 100 g water. These are salt-to-water ratios, not exact final mass percentages. Label cups; keep temperature, water amount, electrode area, spacing and measurement time constant.

05 / Assemble the cell

Place electrodes without touching; connect separate wires above the waterline. Leave cups open and ventilated. Do not apply external power: this is a galvanic cell, not saltwater electrolysis.

06 / Measure open-circuit voltage

Set the meter to DC volts; connect red to copper and black to zinc. Record actual voltage and polarity at a fixed time such as 60 seconds. Voltage alone does not establish motor-driving capability.

07 / Measure loaded power

Place a 1 kΩ resistor across the cell and measure voltage across it. Calculate I=V/R and P=V²/R. Example only: 0.50 V across 1,000 Ω gives 0.50 mA and 0.25 mW. Repeat with 100 Ω if the resistor rating permits. Never connect a current-mode meter directly across the cell.

08 / Repeat controlled trials

Perform at least three trials per solution with equivalent fresh electrode surfaces and consistent timing. Record loaded voltage, calculated current/power, temperature, spacing, exposed area and corrosion. Plot average power and trial ranges. Do not assume more salt always improves performance.

09 / Assess motor feasibility

Compare loaded output against motor specifications, including startup current. Insufficient output is a valid result. Separate cells wired in series can raise voltage but do not guarantee enough current. Measure the complete pack before connecting a motor; start with its bare shaft rather than an exposed propeller.

10 / Revise and document

Change one variable, such as spacing or area, at a time. Print a revised holder and retest. Save CAD, dimensioned drawings, circuit, bill of materials, raw data and photos. Report only measured results.

Bench precautions

Keep the experiment small, ventilated and unsealed because gas may form. Keep away from flames, mains electricity and your MacBook. Do not add acids, bleach or cleaners, and do not charge or externally electrolyze the saltwater. Ask campus lab staff about handling and disposal of metal-containing liquid. Wash hands after handling electrodes.
